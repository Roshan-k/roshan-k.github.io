Eagl

From the first tap to parking the cart.

A golf cart rental app for golf clubs in Norway. A golfer scans a cart’s QR code to ride right away, or books a cart ahead for a date and time, and pays a fixed price or by the minute.

00:03 The brief

Requirements in. Rough screens first.

The product owner provided the flows and documented the requirements for each screen. There were no wireframes, so the flows were worked out directly on screens.

Early explorations, shown as they were, made once the dark direction was chosen. The logo is blurred. The map in these screens was a placeholder; the final design uses the map the client is licensed to use.

Context of use

Where it gets used

Booking happens before the round. Golfers use Eagl outdoors, often in bright sun and with one hand, while moving between holes. On the course, they scan the cart’s QR code, swipe to start, check the timer and end the ride. Those moments use large yellow buttons, a large timer, and short sheets over the map.

00:04 UI decisions

Requirement in, UI decision out.

Seven places where a requirement from the product owner became a specific piece of UI.

a

Swipe to start the ride.

Requirement
Starting a booked ride starts a paid ride.
UI decision
A swipe control: “Swipe when you’re ready to start the ride”. At the end of the swipe it turns green, reads “Unlocked”, and the screen confirms “Cart unlocked successfully”.
Why
To prevent accidental starts of a paid ride.

b

Bottom sheets over the map.

Requirement
Booking, payment, the ride timer and ending the ride.
UI decision
Each step opens as a sheet from the bottom of the screen, with the map still in view behind it.
Why
These are short, supporting actions inside the ride flow, not separate destinations. A sheet lets golfers complete them without extra navigation, so the experience doesn’t feel heavier than it needs to.

c

Three timer states.

Requirement
The ride time, the maximum time and the overtime fee.
UI decision
The timer is active in white; in overtime the time and the word “Overtime” turn red, with the fee per minute; and it collapses into a bar on the map. Fixed price counts down to show the time left; per minute counts up because there is no fixed end.
Why
Active is the main timer while the cart is in use. Overtime turns red because riding past the booked time, such as 6 hours, is charged at a higher rate. The collapsed bar keeps the time visible while the golfer checks the map, so the timer doesn’t always cover the whole screen.

d

Two pricing cards.

Requirement
Two ways to pay: a fixed price or per minute.
UI decision
Two cards side by side under the 9 kr starting fee: Fixed price, 18 holes = 6 hr, 400 kr; and Minute price, 1,6 kr per minute. Each card has an info icon.
Why
Some golfers play a short round and some play a full round, so both options sit side by side for the golfer to pick the one that fits.

e

Rules before commitment.

Requirement
Cancellation charges, battery responsibility and overtime fees.
UI decision
The rules appear in a dialog before the booking or ride continues, one line each, with “Agree” and “Disagree”. The cancellation rule appears again when the golfer cancels a ride.
Why
So the golfer knows every charge before committing: transparent pricing with no hidden charges, and no dispute after the ride. Showing the cancellation rule again at the moment of cancelling means the 50% charge is never a surprise.

f

The end-of-ride checklist.

Requirement
Where to leave the cart, charging and trash.
UI decision
Before the ride ends, a checklist: leave the cart in the designated area, plug in the charger cable, remove all trash and leave the cart as you found it. The golfer taps “I understand”.
Why
So the next golfer gets a clean cart, charged and ready for the next ride.

g

Ride status labels.

Requirement
Ride history with active, upcoming, expired and cancelled rides.
UI decision
A status label on each ride in the list: Active in yellow, Expired in red, Cancelled in grey. An expired ride shows a red “Ride expired” label in its details.
Why
So golfers can see each ride’s state at a glance. Colour and a text label are used together, so no state relies on colour alone.

00:07 Reflection

One thing I’d change now.

Sheets work for short actions like payment or ending a ride. Booking turned out to be seven steps long, so I’d give it its own screens, showing how many steps there are and which one the golfer is on.

When an interaction becomes a multi-step task, it deserves its own focused flow.
