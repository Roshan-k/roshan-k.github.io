TPNL

The Prenatal Nutrition Library

An app that starts from where you are, not from where the content is filed.

A subscription app that helps people eat well from preconception through pregnancy, postpartum and lactation, without Googling every ingredient first. The rebuild changed how the app is organised: from a content library to an experience built around the person’s stage and week.

01 · The problem

Every meal came with a question: is this safe, at this stage, for me and the baby?

People trying to conceive, pregnant or recently postpartum worry about what’s safe and healthy to eat. So they Google, ingredient by ingredient, and what they find hasn’t been checked by a dietitian. Food becomes a source of stress.

The old app already held a dietitian’s answers. But it organised them by where they were stored, not by what someone needed at that moment. That made it a product problem, not a screen problem.

Where this came from

The founder’s own customers: the people the founder works with as a dietitian. The app was made for them.

My role

I owned the design, from information architecture through interaction design to final UI, working directly with the founder, who was the product owner and is a registered dietitian.

How I approached it

This wasn’t a conventional research-led project. I didn’t have access to a recruited research panel or a formal usability study. I worked from customer input shared by the founder, the founder’s domain expertise, a review of comparable health and nutrition products, and the existing product. I treated these as directional evidence, and framed the decisions as hypotheses rather than validated findings.

For each decision I asked four questions:

  1. What is the person trying to do?
  2. What decision are they making?
  3. What do they need to know at that moment?
  4. What can be removed so it takes less effort?

02 · The app before

A library you had to learn before you could use it.

The old app presented the founder’s content the way it was stored: as a library. The structure asked people to know what kind of content held their answer before they could look for it.

  1. Search began by choosing a content type: Search Notes.
  2. One weekly note for everyone: This week’s note: Sprouts.
  3. The bottom bar: Quick Guide, Bookmarks, Community and Meal Plan.
  1. Meal plans were a gallery of fixed plans: 5 Day Meal Plan, Post Partum.
  1. The welcome page relied on two videos, one of them How To Navigate The Library.
  1. Answers sat inside long articles, reached through each article’s own contents menu.

03 · What made it hard

A large library, very different stages, and health at stake.

The founder already had a large body of expert content on the TPNL website. All of it had to come into one app, without overwhelming the person using it.

  • Notes
  • Guides
  • Courses
  • Podcasts
  • 600+ recipes
  • Everything, without overwhelmNotes, guides, courses, podcasts and hundreds of recipes in one app, without burying the one answer someone needs.
  • Very different stagesTrying to conceive, each trimester, postpartum and lactation each ask something different of food.
  • Health content, handled responsiblyExpert-backed, and clear about what an app can’t advise on for one person.
  • Quick answers and whole weeksSome people want to know whether they can eat something now; others want to plan the whole week.

The hardest part

The hardest part wasn’t designing the screens. It was working out, with the founder, how much personalisation the product should offer, without overwhelming people or implying it could replace professional nutrition advice.

That question runs through the information architecture, search, dietary preferences, the recipe screen, the line between community and expert content, and nutrition services.

04 · The shift

From how the content is filed to where you are.

The stage chosen at onboarding, down to the week of pregnancy, now drives the app: Today, the nutrition tip, meal plans, recipes and Learn by Stage.

Problem
The old app asked people to pick a content type before searching, and gave everyone the same weekly note.
Context
Pregnancy keeps changing. What’s safe and useful to eat depends on the stage and the week.
Hypothesis
An app that starts from where someone is will serve them better than a library they have to search.
Direction
Set by the founder: the stage and week drive the whole app.
My design
The stage is chosen at onboarding, easy to change later, and carried through every screen.
Trade-off

05 · Decisions

Four decisions, each on a screen.

The founder, a registered dietitian, set what the app should do and its main flows. The decisions below are about how I made those ideas work on screen. Each one answers the same question from a different side: what should I eat, right now, at this stage?

Decision 01

The recipe as a decision tool

Problem
A recipe tells you how to cook something, not whether it’s right for you now.
Hypothesis
A recipe can support several decisions at once: is this for me, is it right for my stage, how much do I need, what do I buy, and do I want it in my plan.
Direction
Set by the founder: recipes should show why they suit your stage.
My design
The why-it’s-good box sits above the ingredients, and the two actions stay fixed at the bottom.
Why
The question people bring is about their stage. The recipe answers it before the ingredient list starts.
Trade-off
Some visual simplicity. The screen is more information-dense than a recipe on its own, but people can decide and take the next step without leaving it.
On the screen
  • A highlighted box on why key ingredients matter
  • Stage categories: Third Trimester, Postpartum
  • A serving scaler: ½, 1×, 2×, 3×
  • Two actions: Add to Grocery, Add to Meal Plan

Decision 02

Search by how you feel and what you need

Problem
Searching by dish name only helps if you already know what to cook.
Hypothesis
People may not know the name of the recipe they want, but they know their stage, the meal they need and how they feel.
Direction
Set by the founder: search by stage, meal and symptom.
My design
Filters grouped by stage, meal and symptom as chips; the chosen filters stay visible on the results.
Why
It lets search start from what someone already knows about their situation.
Trade-off
Some of the simplicity of a plain search box. More controls, in exchange for starting from how someone feels instead of needing to know the recipe.
The filters
  • Stage, including gestational diabetes
  • Meal, from breakfast to sides
  • Symptoms: Good for Nausea, Constipation, Heartburn, Headaches, Swelling
  • The results keep the chosen filters in view: Second Trimester, Lunch, Good for Heartburn

Decision 03

Guardrails inside personalisation

Problem
Every dietary restriction makes nutrient needs harder to meet.
Hypothesis
Explaining the consequence will inform people better than a hard limit, without taking the choice away.
Direction
Set by the founder: warn when too many dietary restrictions are chosen.
My design
A warning instead of a block, shown when a fourth preference is tapped.
Why
Preferences are personal. The app’s job is to flag the nutrition risk, not to overrule the choice.
Trade-off
Some control over how predictable the personalised results are. A warning instead of a block keeps the final choice with the user, without extra friction.
In the preference picker
  • Tapping a fourth dietary preference brings up “Heads up!”: more than 3 may limit recipe options and make nutrient needs harder to meet
  • After “I Understand”, the fourth is selected: the app warns, it doesn’t block
  • Preferences can be changed at any time
  • “No preferences” turns the other options off

Decision 04

Clear about where advice ends

Problem
Health questions invite personal answers that an app shouldn’t give.
Hypothesis
Community discussion and professional help serve different needs, and people should always know which one they are in.
Direction
Set by the founder: be clear about what the app can’t advise on.
My design
The HIPAA note sits at the top of the forum, above every topic.
Why
Expert content stays trustworthy when its limits are stated.
Trade-off
Some simplicity: the note adds text to the forum, and Nutrition services is an extra step away. In return, community discussion and professional nutrition help stay clearly apart.
Where it shows
  • Ask an RD is a community forum for general questions. A note says it isn’t HIPAA compliant, so people shouldn’t post lab work or medical records
  • Nutrition services is a separate, professional path, reached from the menu: HTMA Bundle, Private Coaching and Quick Question Call

10 · What I learned

Two things I took from it.

  • Information architecture is about timing, not just categories

    The question wasn’t only where content belonged. It was when someone needed it, and what decision they were making. Hundreds of recipes, notes, guides, courses and podcasts don’t become useful by being categorised. Starting from the stage and week gives people a more relevant place to begin.

  • Personalisation needs boundaries

    The product has to help without pretending to replace individual professional advice. Here that meant a warning when dietary preferences stack up, and a clear line between community discussion, expert content and nutrition services, so it is visible where the app’s advice ends.

11 · What I’d validate next

Hypotheses, not results.

None of these was tested after launch. If I continued the project, these are the questions I would investigate first.

  • 01 · Stage-first navigationDoes starting from the pregnancy stage help people find relevant content with less effort than browsing the library?
  • 02 · Recipe actionsDo people understand and use Add to Grocery and Add to Meal Plan when they are viewing a recipe?
  • 03 · Contextual searchWhich combination of stage, meal type and symptoms actually supports discovery?
  • 04 · Personalisation guardrailsDoes the warning help people understand the consequence of choosing too many preferences, without creating unnecessary friction?
